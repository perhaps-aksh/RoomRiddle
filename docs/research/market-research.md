## 1. Executive summary

RoomRiddle AI is a cinematic, multiplayer mystery game designed for the living room. The television becomes a shared crime scene; players join from their phones to examine clues, question suspects, share theories and solve the case together.

It sits between three things households already do: watching TV (cinematic but passive), playing puzzle games (active but solitary, on phones), and board or party games (social but with setup and rules). The proposition:

> **A mystery adventure that brings people together to solve a case, rather than simply watch a story unfold.**

### Key conclusions

1. **Primary audience (hypothesis):** adults 18–34 who enjoy mystery, puzzle, detective and cooperative entertainment.
2. **Secondary audience:** families, teenagers, couples and friend groups.
3. **Platform focus:** stay on Fire TV / Vega OS for the hackathon rather than spreading across platforms.
4. **Differentiation:** the TV is the cinematic shared investigation; phones are personal controllers and private clue screens.
5. **AI with a job, inside the rules:** AI voices the suspects and the narrator and rephrases hints, but only from an authored truth table and only what the room has earned. A deterministic engine decides every answer; every case is solvable without AI.
6. **Family impact:** cooperative mysteries can create opportunities for conversation and shared experiences; positive relationship outcomes are not guaranteed.
7. **Monetization:** a free introductory case, then paid mystery packs. Subscription only after repeat play is proven.
8. **Content strategy:** a growing library of distinct cases on one reusable engine. Five cases are designed.
9. **Play-time hypothesis:** test 7–9 PM local time and weekends. Not an established peak.
10. **Next milestone:** test with real players and measure completion, phone joining, replay intent and willingness to pay before any revenue forecast.

## 2. Product overview

### 2.1 What RoomRiddle AI is

A TV-first interactive detective game. A typical session:

1. A cinematic introduction on the TV.
2. Players join by scanning a QR code; no app install and no account.
3. A narrator (detective, curator or guardian, depending on the case) frames the question.
4. Players explore a 2.5D scene, inspect objects and discover evidence; some clues are private to one phone.
5. Players question suspects in natural language; each answers in character and only with what the evidence allows.
6. Puzzles require observation, logic or combining evidence. Three progressive hints are always available.
7. The group submits a theory; the case is explained with the clues that prove it.
8. A completed-case summary and an invitation to the next case.

### 2.2 Product principles

| Principle | Product implication |
|---|---|
| TV-first | The main scene, story and investigation stay on the television. |
| Multiplayer | Up to six phones join one synchronized room. |
| Cooperative | Shared reasoning and group results, not individual ranking. |
| Cinematic | Scenes, camera movement, sound, lighting and dialogue create atmosphere. |
| Expandable | New mysteries are data on one engine, not new apps. |
| AI-enhanced, AI-independent | AI makes characters and hints feel alive; it never decides the game. |
| Accessible | Remote-only control, large type, subtitles, reduced motion, untimed family cases. |
| Family-aware | Age labels, content notes and cooperative mechanics for mixed-age groups. |

### 2.3 Current build (October 2026)

Implemented and tested: the deterministic engine, the authoritative multiplayer server with QR join and reconnection, the phone companion, the Vega OS TV app with remote navigation, a 2.5D cinematic world, a data-driven audio system, and The Last Train as a complete case. In progress: truth tables, four more cases, the AI layer, final art and sound. Planned: hardware validation on a Fire TV Stick, and a downloadable mystery library. Technical detail is in the project README.

## 3. Market opportunity

### 3.1 Where RoomRiddle AI sits

At the intersection of connected-TV entertainment, casual and puzzle gaming, interactive storytelling, social and cooperative gaming, family entertainment, and detective fiction. The opportunity includes households that do not call themselves gamers but want something more interactive than a film and less demanding than a console game. Adjacent markets do not prove demand for this product; it needs its own validation.

## 4. Target audience strategy

### 4.1 Primary: adults 18–34 (hypothesis to test first)

Motivations: challenging puzzles, detective and thriller stories, playing with friends or a partner, something different on the TV, interactive narrative, discovering new cases over time.

Product implications: strong premises, layered clues and satisfying deductions, cinematic presentation, moderate suspense, 15–30 minute cases, effortless phone joining, optional harder puzzles, distinct settings.

### 4.2 Secondary: families

The TV is already a shared screen, and phones give each person a way to contribute: family game nights, weekends, parent–child play, siblings, and multigenerational groups. Family cases must be designed for cooperation, age suitability and ease of play, not just made easier.

### 4.3 Content hypotheses by age

| Age | Motivation | Content direction |
|---|---|---|
| 4–7 | Playful discovery | Very simple visual mysteries with adult support |
| 8–12 | Adventure, codes, discovery | Missing objects, secret messages, symbol puzzles |
| 13–17 | Suspense, complex reasoning | Layered clues, technology mysteries, non-graphic suspense |
| 18–24 | Social play, thrillers | Heists, technology cases, unreliable witnesses |
| 25–44 | Story-led group activity | Classic whodunits, historical intrigue |
| 45–64 | Complex, accessible stories | Historical cases, espionage, cold cases |
| 65+ | Comfortable, story-led play | Cosy mysteries, readable clues, no time pressure |

These guide content testing; they are not measured preferences, and individuals vary.

### 4.4 Testing order

1. Adults 18–34 who like mysteries or puzzles.
2. Couples and friend groups.
3. Mixed-age family groups.
4. Clearly labelled family cases.
5. Expand the catalogue using completion and repeat-play data.

## 5. Mystery catalogue and content strategy

RoomRiddle AI offers several distinct cases, each with its own setting, characters, central question, evidence, puzzles and conclusion. Three to four puzzles per case is the starting point for testing.

### 5.1 The five designed cases

| # | Case | Audience | Length | Premise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Last Train** (flagship, playable) | Teens & adults | 15–25 min | A storm, a held night train, a vanished conductor and something that happened at 11:47 PM. Players read clocks, rebuild a torn ticket, expose a forged ledger entry and stop the train before it is sent into a sealed tunnel. |
| 2 | **The Hollow Manor** | Teens & adults | 20–30 min | A historian vanishes from a locked study. A moved portrait, an old floor plan and an altered catalogue reveal a forgotten passage and a race to protect a family record. |
| 3 | **Signal Lost** | Teens & adults | 20–30 min | A remote communications station goes silent after a warning. A fast clock and a timing fault turn an apparent threat into a protective shutdown. |
| 4 | **The Forgotten Temple** | Family, 8+ | 15–25 min | Four carved symbols lead to an archive hidden to protect manuscripts from floods. Ends with a preservation decision, not a treasure grab. |
| 5 | **The Yaksha's Three Questions** | All ages | 10–20 min | Inspired by the Yaksha Prashna episode of the Mahabharata, with original puzzles: observe before deciding, consider consequences, combine what everyone noticed. Untimed, no penalties, respectful content note. |

Each case has stable ids, deterministic answers, three authored hints per puzzle (nudge, connect, explain), a truth table for its characters, and an explanation that connects the evidence to the solution. The rules: no unavoidable softlocks, no clue the final answer needs that players cannot find, wrong answers prompt another try rather than punishment.

### 5.2 Future collections (concepts only)

- **Family:** The Missing Birthday Gift, The Secret of the School Clock, The Midnight Museum.
- **Teen:** The Locked School Laboratory, The Anonymous Message, The Hidden Server.
- **Adult detective:** The Locked-Room Incident, The Missing Witness, The False Alibi.
- **Technology:** The Smart-House Incident, The Vanishing Digital Evidence (no real-world attack instructions).
- **Historical / atmospheric:** The Clockmaker's Secret, The Lighthouse Keeper, The Archive of Missing Letters.

### 5.3 Why variety matters

One strong case attracts players once; a library gives them a reason to return. Variety supports repeat play, word of mouth, paid packs, seasonal releases, different difficulty levels and new marketing moments. Each case also costs money to make, so reusable scene templates, puzzle types, asset pipelines and automated content validation keep the cost per case down.

## 6. Competitive landscape and differentiation

RoomRiddle AI competes for a household's evening, not only with identical games.

| Category | What users already get | RoomRiddle AI opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Streaming services | Passive cinematic entertainment | Viewers become investigators |
| Mobile puzzle games | Convenient solo play | A shared TV experience |
| Console and party games (e.g. phone-controlled party packs) | Group play, quick rounds | Deduction, evidence and narrative instead of trivia or drawing |
| Board games | Face-to-face play | No setup, no rule book, new cases on demand |
| Murder-mystery kits | Structured group storytelling | Interactive scenes, replayable, questionable suspects |
| Hidden-object games | Visual search | Cooperative discussion and case-level deduction |
| Quiz and trivia | Accessible competition | Reasoning from evidence, not general knowledge |

These are category comparisons; a product-by-product audit (prices, ratings, availability, reviews) is still to do.

### What RoomRiddle AI should be known for

1. A cinematic TV investigation.
2. Phone-based multiplayer with private clues, so the group has to talk.
3. Suspects you can actually question, who only know what they should.
4. Distinct, downloadable cases.
5. A narrator who reacts to discoveries.
6. A clear ending that explains why the evidence leads to the solution.

The message is not "AI mystery game". It is:

> **A cinematic detective adventure where everyone in the living room helps solve the case.**

## 8. Family relationships and social impact

### 8.1 Can RoomRiddle AI improve family bonding?

Potentially, if it is designed for cooperation. The evidence is encouraging but qualified:
- **2018, *New Media & Society*** (361 parents): more frequent family video-game co-play was associated with greater family satisfaction and closeness. Observational and self-reported; it does not show causation.
- **2025, *Family Relations*** (30 children, parents and grandparents): intergenerational board gaming was perceived to help communication, shared experience, understanding and closeness. Small qualitative sample.
- Research on gaming and social support is mixed.

**Implication:** RoomRiddle AI can create opportunities for meaningful shared time. Whether it improves relationships must be measured, not promised.

### 8.2 How cooperative mysteries can help
- **Shared problem-solving:** comparing observations and building one explanation.
- **Cross-generational contribution:** a child spots a visual detail; an adult catches a contradiction. Nobody is assumed better at a task because of age.
- **Listening and communication:** players explain reasoning and revise it when evidence changes.
- **Shared memories:** a twist, a funny reaction or a hard-won answer becomes something to talk about later.
- **Cooperative achievement:** the group wins together.

### 8.3 Risks
One person controlling everything; children unable to contribute; competitive scoring causing arguments; unclear clues; one player hogging the remote; sessions that run too long; frightening content; confusing setup.

### 8.4 Features that support healthy group play

| Feature | Benefit |
|---|---|
| Rotating lead detective | Everyone gets a turn to lead |
| Shared evidence board (case journal) | Keeps the group on common facts |
| Optional private clues per phone | Players must share what they found |
| Many ways to contribute | Observation, deduction, codes, questioning suspects |
| Cooperative completion result | Rewards the group, not the fastest player |
| No-blame ending | Explains the evidence without mocking wrong theories |
| Age labels and content notes | Families choose suitable cases |
| Natural stopping points | Easy to pause and finish |
| Post-case prompt: "Which clue changed your mind?" | Discussion about reasoning, not just being right |

### 8.5 Family Mystery Night (signature mode concept)
1. The narrator introduces the case on the TV.
2. Everyone joins by phone; the game hands out roles or private clues.
3. The group investigates the shared scene and questions suspects.
4. Players discuss and submit one theory.
5. The narrator reveals and explains the solution.
6. A cooperative result, then the next case or a saved point.

## 9. Monetization strategy

Monetize the growing library, not entry: players should understand the experience before paying.

### 9.1 Models

| Model | Advantages | Risks | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free introductory case | Removes uncertainty | Free users may not convert | Launch with it |
| Paid mystery packs | Clear value; fits episodic content | Needs consistently good cases | **Primary model to test** |
| Individual cases | Low commitment | Lower basket size | After pack pricing is tested |
| Premium bundle | Larger purchase | Needs catalogue depth | As the library grows |
| Subscription | Recurring revenue | Needs steady content and retention | Defer until repeat play is proven |
| Cosmetics (themes) | Revenue without selling solutions | Distraction | Low priority |
| Seasonal cases | Reason to return | Production burden | After launch |

Payments must follow the Amazon Appstore's in-app purchasing rules and fees, verified before implementation.

### 9.2 Price hypotheses (to test, not market prices)

| Product | Test price |
|---|---:|
| Introductory case | Free |
| Small pack | US$2.99 |
| Larger pack | US$4.99–7.99 |
| Premium bundle | US$9.99–14.99 |
| Future subscription | US$3.99 / month |

Local purchasing power, store fees, taxes and currency affect final prices.

### 9.3 Structure and principles
- **Free:** one polished case (The Last Train).
- **Pack A / Pack B:** related cases sharing a theme or difficulty.
- **Bundle:** multiple packs at a discount.
- Never sell essential hints, and never make the free case deliberately frustrating. Hints stay free; that is part of the cooperative promise.

### 9.4 When to add a subscription
Only with strong repeat play, a regular release cadence, a catalogue that justifies recurring payment, predictable production capacity, and transparent renewal and cancellation.

## 11. Technical strategy for an expandable library

Summary (full architecture and diagrams in the README):

- **Engine and content are separate.** The TV app contains the engine, renderer, audio and supported puzzle types. A case is data: scenes, clues, puzzles, hints, endings, truth tables, and asset references.
- **No downloaded code.** A pack can only use mechanics the installed engine supports. New mechanics ship in app updates.
- **Catalogue + packs.** A catalogue API lists cases and versions; packs (manifest + assets with checksums and a minimum engine version) come from object storage / CDN; the device caches them for offline play.
- **Server authority.** The multiplayer server loads the same pack version and decides every answer.
- **Purchases are entitlements** verified with the store, separate from the downloaded files.
- **Reliability:** cached cases, local puzzle logic, resumable downloads, clear offline messages, versioned content, no lost progress on disconnect.

A candidate backend for the catalogue is managed PostgreSQL with object storage (for example Supabase); this must be validated against the Vega SDK's networking and storage before commitment.

## 12. Where AI fits

AI makes the investigation feel alive; it is never the authority.

| Feature | Role | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Suspect interrogation | Players ask suspects anything; they answer in character | Only speakable facts from the truth table; spoiler terms blocked until earned; authored fallback |
| Adaptive hints | Rephrase the next authored hint level for the group's situation | Never beyond the current level; never marks a puzzle solved |
| Narrator reactions | The detective / curator / Yaksha reacts to discoveries and theories | Grounded in found evidence |
| Voice questions | Phone microphone → transcription → question | Same gates as typed questions |
| Content tooling (development only) | Draft and check new cases | Human-authored truth is final |

**Free-tier design:** Gemini Flash first, Groq as fallback, authored lines last; keys on the server only; a per-room question budget, short replies and caching. Players never pay for AI and the game stays fully playable without it.

**Safety:** puzzle correctness stays deterministic; the AI only sees the case data the room has earned; outputs are validated; age-appropriate and culturally sensitive topics are listed per case as off-limits; no personal data is sent.

## 13. Launch and user validation plan

### 13.1 First test group
20–30 people: mystery and puzzle fans, casual players, couples and friends, families, and people new to the concept. Good for usability issues and recurring reactions; not statistically representative.

### 13.2 Metrics

| Metric | Tells us |
|---|---|
| Time to first puzzle | How fast people reach the real experience |
| Phone join success | Whether multiplayer setup works |
| Case start / completion rate | Pacing and satisfaction |
| Puzzle abandonment | Where people get stuck |
| Hint usage | Clue clarity; value of adaptive hints |
| Questions asked of suspects | Whether interrogation is used and helpful |
| Session length | Fit with expectations |
| Repeat-play intent and actual repeat play | Retention signal |
| Purchase intent and price preference | Which pricing to test properly |
| Group participation | Whether everyone contributes |
| Family feedback | Discussion versus frustration |

### 13.3 Provisional success criteria (internal, not benchmarks)
Most testers start the first case without help; phone joining works on test devices; testers understand what to investigate; a meaningful share finish without outside help; players can explain the solution from the clues; groups report discussion; some clearly want another case; pricing feedback identifies at least one structure worth a real test. Set numeric targets after the first round.

### 13.4 Pricing questions
Would you play another case? One free case and paid packs, or individual cases, or a bundle? What price feels fair for the content? Would you subscribe for regular releases? What would make you stop paying? (Stated intent is not behaviour; confirm later with a real purchase test.)

## 14. Risks and mitigation

| Risk | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Setup friction | Players quit before starting | QR join, no install, one-press start |
| Small catalogue | No reason to return | Five cases at launch; data-driven production |
| Weak puzzle logic | Solutions feel arbitrary | Automated validation + fresh-player tests |
| AI hallucination or spoilers | Wrong or premature information | Truth tables, spoiler guards, deterministic engine, fallback lines |
| AI free-tier limits | Slow or missing replies | Provider fallback, caching, per-room budget, authored lines |
| Unstable network | Can't start or finish | Reconnection, host pause, cached content |
| Device fragmentation | Inconsistent behaviour | Test on target devices; declare compatibility |
| Long sessions | Families abandon | 10–30 min cases, natural stops |
| Competitive conflict | Arguments | Cooperative results, no-blame endings |
| Unsuitable content | Upset players | Age labels, content notes, gentle family cases |
| Cultural insensitivity | Offence, loss of trust | Respectful treatment, clear fiction labels, content notes, review |
| Unproven monetization | Low conversion | Test free-to-paid before scaling |
| Production cost | Each case too expensive | Reusable pipelines, AI-assisted art, validation tooling |
| Overstated market | Misleading projections | Model reachable compatible users; validate acquisition |

## 15. Priorities

1. **A polished core game:** correct logic, reliable interactions, art and audio, loading and error handling, effortless joining, clean completion and replay.
2. **An expandable content system:** multiple cases, stable ids, manifests, versioning, caching, later an online catalogue.
3. **Controlled AI:** interrogation and adaptive hints bound by truth tables, then voice input.
4. **Audience validation:** adults, couples, friends and families; observe participation.
5. **Monetization tests:** one free case and one paid pack before any subscription.
6. **Expand on evidence:** let completion, retention and purchase data choose the next cases.

## 16. Recommendation

Position RoomRiddle AI as **a cinematic cooperative mystery game for Fire TV that turns the living room into an interactive detective experience**, combining a polished free case, a small varied catalogue of paid cases, phone-based cooperative play, a distinctive 2.5D presentation, deterministic puzzle logic, suspects and hints powered by grounded AI, a content architecture for future releases, and family options without making the whole product a children's game.

The opportunity combines established behaviours: watching TV, solving puzzles, playing games and spending time together. The size of the TV market does not prove demand for this product. The open questions:

1. Will people understand and enjoy a TV-first mystery game?
2. Will phone joining feel effortless?
3. Will groups finish a case and want another?
4. Will families find it genuinely cooperative?
5. Will enough players pay for more cases?
6. Can new cases be produced economically and consistently?

A polished prototype and structured user testing answer these better than large theoretical figures.

**Long-term vision:** a growing library of cinematic mysteries that friends, couples and families return to whenever they want to solve a case together.

## Appendix B — Formulas

- **Paid-pack gross revenue** = annual buyers × packs per buyer × average pack price.
- **Subscription gross revenue** = average paying subscribers × monthly price × 12.
- **Net operating contribution** = gross revenue − platform fees − taxes and refunds − content production − infrastructure − marketing − other costs.
- **Purchase conversion** = paying users ÷ eligible users × 100.
- **Case completion rate** = completed cases ÷ started cases × 100.
- **Repeat-play rate** = users who return within the period ÷ users in the cohort × 100.
